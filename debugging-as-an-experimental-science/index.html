Everyone is debugging

Debugging is a universal experience for anyone writing code. It's usually considered as annoying, or even frustrating, one of those things you have to do, you know, no job is perfect. Sometimes, it can feel like wandering blind in a maze, trying to get out. After some time, you're not sure if you have already tried that, or not. And this way, was it really a dead end or did you miss something?

There is a logical explanation to bugs.

Code is behaving rationally, and there is a logical explanation to bugs. We can use a systematic approach to find the cause. There is something called the scientific method, that has had a few good results in the past. Could we use that?

Approaching debugging as

an experimental science

Tada!

Experimental science = theory + experiments

The scientific methodology is based on two interdependent pillars: theory and experiments. Observation -> theory -> experiments. Theory should be validated by experiments, and experiments are guided by the theory.
I was never taught how to debug. For a long time, I wasn't using a principled approach, I was iterating until my code worked. It would be like wandering at random: kind of work for small mazes, but it's frustrating and solving one bug is not helping you solve the next one.
So I started to take notes. Virtually putting marks on walls to avoid going in circles. Then I mapped my path, to be better the next time I was in the same region of the maze (have the same bug). And I realized my goal has changed.

Don't try to fix your bug

Understand what happens

I don't want to fix my bug by chance, and forever be haunted by the fear of it coming back. I want to understand what happened, so I can build on this knowledge to solve more complex cases. I want to be able to transfer this knowledge to my coworkers, so they can solve their own mazes. I want to know more about Python in general, and my codebase in particular, and being able to share that I've learned. And seeing things that way, debugging is not longer a chore, but it's actually an exciting puzzle to solve. Let's get started to improve your skills for solving mazes efficiently. And we'll start with drawing your map.

Drawing your map

Aka building your mental model of your code. A lot of big words, here, but it's not as bad as it sounds. We'll go step by step.

Drawing your map

You have assumptions about how your code is working

Make them explicit

Focus on the most obvious at first

First, you do have some assumptions about your code expected behaviour. Not high-level, but more detailed. Eg: how an algorithm is registered in Substra. Make them explicit, because we want to be able to test them (and maybe invalidate them). Now, you have too many things to keep track of, how much details should you go into? Start from the beginning, with simple explanations / assumptions, close to where the bug is observed. It might come from earlier. Occam's razor: the simplest explanation is the most plausible. So it's okay to assume at first that Python is not buggy, the hardware is not hit by a rogue neutrino, ...

Drawing your map

Divide and conquer

Divide and conquer is a programming paradigm: rather than trying to solve a complex problem all at once, you split it in smaller problems, solve them, and then combine the solutions. Understand small chunks (components) of your codebase, and how they interact. You might be able to determine which parts of your codebase are involved in the bug, and focus on those at first. There might be several components involved, but understanding them one at a time is easier.

Drawing your map

Use documentation and architecture diagrams

But don't trust them

Preexisting documentation can help you make sense of the code, but use those past maps with a healthy dose of skepticism: they can be outdated or not covering all edge cases. Should be listed in your explicit assumptions: according to the documentation, X is true, and then confirmed with an experiment.

Using your map

Make some hypotheses about what is happening during the bug

It's all good and well to have a map of your surroundings, but what you really want is get out of this mess. With your current mental model, you probably have some hypotheses about what has happened (== which path you took in the maze). Make them explicit as well.

Using your map

Follow the flow

If you have no idea about what is happening, follow the flow of execution, from the input to the output. Start from the input and follow the code flow by reading your code (modern IDE can help). You can also use tools like a debugger to follow the code flow.

Remember

At least one of your assumptions is wrong

The theory is a mental image of your code. You will use it to create hypotheses about what happened, consciously or not. Do it consciously, to be able to challenge your assumptions. If you have a bug, at least one of your assumptions about how your code is working is wrong. All models are wrong, but some are useful (George Box). Now, use your mental model to design experiments, and confirm or infirm your hypotheses.

Finding your way

We have a draft map, let's use it to explore, and fill in the blanks. And then to actually solve your bug and get out of the maze.

Finding your way

Experiments:

What is actually happening when you run your code

Experiments are what you get when confronting your theory to reality. You get information about what happened, from your logs, your outputs, your stack trace, maybe even your monitoring tools if you are lucky. Collecting experimental data points also guide your theory building, it's an iterative process. Some people are more comfortable building an abstract view of the problem first; others prefer to start from concrete logs and stack traces. Both approaches are valid, and you will often use both alternatively in complex bugs.

Finding your way

Experiments are used to test your hypotheses

You can make prediction out of your mental model: if I turn in this direction, the next turn will be to the right. By changing your inputs or your code, you get need data points that can help you confirm or infirm your hypotheses and your assumptions. Good experiments are reproducible, and allow fast iterations.

Finding your way

Be systematic

Trust nothing

Check everything

Print values of things you're unsure about; print values of things you're sure about. If you're sure about something, getting experimental validation is still important. Most frustrating bugs are caused by a wrong "obvious" assumption that is not tested. Be rigorous: something kind of right is not right. In practice...

Finding your way

Inspect values at interfaces.

It's usually easy to inspect values at interfaces: HTTP requests between frontend and backend, SQL queries sent by your ORM to your database. Bonus: "divide and conquer": you're looking for a needle in a haystack: try to make the haystack as small as possible.

Finding your way

Focus on data, not the code flow.

Rather than following what is happening in the code with logs, it's usually more informative to check how your data is changing at each step: print statements, debugger, or just checking the tables in the DB. It's hard to keep track of all states, side effects... One major limitation of your mental model Focusing your experimental approach on data complements nicely your mental model.

Remember

It's all about putting your theory to the test.

Experiments are key to invalidate some of your assumptions. Having a good mental model will help you to design efficient experiments (informative, little effort) Focusing on the state of data helps pinpointing discrepancies quickly.

Keeping track of your progress

It's easy to get lost in the maze of your code, and forget what you've tried, what you've learned. It's important to keep track of your progress, to avoid going in circles.

How?

Scientists use a research log

Scientists use a research log to keep track of all their ideas, experiments, results, ...

Debugging log

  • Write down experiment settings and results
  • Write down your assumptions and hypotheses explicitly
  • Write down ideas you can't follow right now

We can steal the idea and use a similar tool for debugging. First, write all inputs and results of your experiments, to avoid going in circles. Especially important if your experiments are slow. Making your theory explicit is important, because you can better see blind spots: both assumption about how your code works and hypotheses about what is happening during the bug. Ideas you have, even random / stupid ones, as if you were brainstorming.
Iterative process

Debugging log is great to write incident retrospectives

What is happening

  • Default arguments are instantiated once.
  • If a default argument is changed, the new value will be used as default for subsequent calls.
    • beginning.extend(l)
  • DO NOT use mutable default arguments.
Default arguments are created once, when the function is defined. If a default argument is changed during the function call, the new value will be used as default for subsequent calls. It is recommended not to use mutable default arguments. (Mutable == that can be modified, things like list and dict; immutable, tuples, strings and integers)

Other useful tools for your adventure

For the rest of the talk, I'd like to cover more practical advice on how to tackle a real bug, when your code base is not 10-line long, but more like 100k lines. How to build your theoretical view of your code, how to test your hypotheses efficiently, what tools can help you for your experiments... and how you can leverage your debugging log to guide you through your exploration.

Other useful tools for your adventure

Qualify the bug

Leverage temporality

Identify sources of randomness

Invest in tooling

Enroll another adventurer

Architect your maze better

Qualify the bug: what is the bug? Is it a bug? Is it reproducible? Expected vs observed behaviour. Leverage temporality: when was the last it was working? What changed in between? git bisect Identify sources of randomness: control what you can, be aware of what you don't. Beware of side-effects. Invest in tooling: learn how to use a debugger, set up some monitoring. Enroll another adventurer: debugging is a team effort. Synthesize your current level of knowledge to share with a coworker. Architect your maze better: write code easy to debug. code that is easy to test (stateless (FP), small & well-isolated components)

The science of debugging

  • There's a logical explanation.
  • Do not fix the bug, understand what happens.
  • Trust nothing, check all your assumptions.
  • Use a debugging log to track your progress.
