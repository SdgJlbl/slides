Everyone is debugging

CC-BY-SA 4.0, Rolf Kranz

Debugging is a universal experience for anyone writing code. It's often viewed as painful, confusing. But it shouldn't be akin to wandering blind in a labyrinth.

There is a logical explanation to bugs.

At least as long as we are not doing quantum programming, code is behaving rationally, and there is a logical explanation to bugs. We can use a systematic approach to find the cause. We can borrow from the scientific method.

Approaching debugging as an experimental science

Debugging should be approached as an experimental science. We can apply the same method as any scientist trying to understand what is happening.

Experimental science = theory + experiments

It's also that the theory should be validated by experiments, and experiments are guided by the theory.
Typical approach to debugging (iterate until your code works) would be like wandering at random: kind of work for small mazes, but it's frustrating and solving one bug is not helping you solve the next one. Two options: putting marks on wall to avoid going in circles (experimental-based) or trying to draw a map of the maze (theory-based). For complex bugs (large mazes), you probably need to combine both.

Don't try to fix your bug

Understand what happens

If you fix your bug by chance, you're not sure it won't happen again, you won't be able to build on the previous fix to solve more complex cases, you can't transfer knowledge. If you understand what happened, you're learning about your codebase and the idiosyncrasies of your programming language. Debugging becomes an opportunity to learn, instead of a cause of despair. Improve your skills to solve future mazes more efficiently!

Drawing your map

Aka building your mental model of your code. It sounds daunting, but it will be an iterative process.

Drawing your map

You have assumptions about how your code is working

Make them explicit

Focus on the most obvious at first

We are not going to explain the whole of the universe, but focus on a small part we have control over, namely your code. We are making assumptions about how the code is working, and what is happening during the flow of execution. Typically, we assume a few things, like Python is not buggy, the hardware is not hit by a rogue neutrino, ... We'll come back to this, but applying Occam's razor, ie the simplest explanation is the most plausible, is important. Might seem daunting, but it's okay not to list all the assumptions you are making, focus on the most obvious at first. The ones that are most likely to be wrong, are probably the most interesting to test. Start with simple explanations / assumptions, and move to more complex ones if needed. Remember, it's a back and forth between theory and experiments.

Drawing your map

Divide and conquer

One good option to make the mapping of your codebase less daunting is to divide and conquer. Well separated components help. If you have a large codebase, you can start by identifying the components that are involved in the bug. Then, you can focus on one component at a time. When you inherit a legacy codebase, you don't always have this luxury. Still, start from a general view, and zoom in on your portion of the map / codebase.

Drawing your map

Use documentation and architecture diagrams

But don't trust them

Use past "maps" with a healthy dose of skepticism. What you want to do is building a good high-level view of your code / a mental model of what is happening Easier with experience, for codebase you know well, takes time to build. Preexisting documentation can help you make sense of the code, but can be outdated or not covering all edge cases. Should be listed in your explicit assumptions: according to the documentation, X is true, and then confirmed with an experiment.

Using your map

Make some hypotheses about what is happening during the bug

It's all good and well to have a map of your surroundings, but what you really want is get out of this mess. With your current mental model, you probably have some hypotheses about what has happened (== which path you took in the maze). Make them explicit as well.

Using your map

Follow the flow

If you have no idea about what is happening, follow the flow of execution, from the input to the output. Start from the input and follow the code flow by reading your code (modern IDE can help). You can also use a debugger to follow the code flow.

Wrapping up: theory

Remember: at least one of your assumptions is wrong

The theory is a mental image of your code. You will use it to create hypotheses about what happened, consciously or not. Do it consciously, to be able to challenge your assumptions. If you have a bug / unexpected behaviour, at least one of your assumptions about how your code is working is wrong. All models are wrong, but some are useful (George Box). Your experiments must help you confirm or infirm your hypotheses.

Tracing your way

Having a map of your code is a start, but you probably need to explore as well, first to be able to draw your map, and then to actually solve your bug and get out of the maze.

Tracing your way

Designing and recording experiments:

What is actually happening when you run your code

Experiments are what you get when confronting your theory to reality. You get information about what happened, from your logs, your outputs, your stack trace, maybe even your monitoring tools if you are lucky. Collecting experimental data points also guide your theory building. Some people are more comfortable building an abstract view of the problem first; others prefer to start from concrete logs and stack traces. Both approaches are valid, and you will often use both alternatively in complex bugs.

Tracing your way

Experiments are used to test your hypotheses

You can make prediction out of your mental model: if I turn in this direction, the next turn will be to the right. By changing your inputs or your code, you get need data points that can help you confirm or infirm your hypotheses and your assumptions. Be rigorous: something sligthly off is not right.

Tracing your way

Be systematic

Trust nothing

Check everything

Print values of things you're unsure about. Print values of things you're sure about. If you're sure about something, getting experimental validation is still important. Most frustrating bugs are caused by a wrong "obvious" assumption that is not tested. Be systematic.

Tracing your way

Inspect values at interfaces.

Back to "divide and conquer": you're looking for a needle in a haystack: try to make the haystack as small as possible. Even if your codebase is not neatly divided into components, you can still identify interfaces (between backend / frontend, server and db). Use the web console to inspect HTTP requests between frontend and backend. Inspect SQL queries sent by your ORM to your database. It's often a convenient place to get information, and it provides a lot of value to identify where the problem is (in js or in Python or in SQL).

Tracing your way

Focus on data, not the code flow.

One major limitation of your mental model is probably unaccounted-for side effects. It's hard to keep track of all states, and some might be well hidden. Rather than following what is happening in the code with logs, it's usually more informative to check how your data is changing at each step: print statements, debugger, or just checking the values in the DB.

Wrapping up: experiments

It's all about putting your theory to the test.

Experiments are here to invalidate some of your assumptions. Having a good mental model will help you to design efficient experiments (informative, little effort) Focusing on the state of data helps pinpointing discrepancies quickly.

Keeping track of your progress

It's easy to get lost in the maze of your code, and forget what you've tried, what you've learned. It's important to keep track of your progress, to avoid going in circles.

How?

Scientists use a research log

Scientists use a research log to keep track of all their ideas, experiments, results, ...

Debugging log

  • Write down experiment settings and results
  • Write down your assumptions and hypotheses explicitly
  • Write down ideas you can't follow right now
We can steal the idea and use a similar tool for debugging. First, write all inputs and results of your experiments, to avoid going in circles. Especially important if your experiments are slow. Making your theory explicit is important, because you can better see blind spots: both assumption about how your code works and hypotheses about what is happening during the bug. Ideas you have, even random / stupid ones, as if you were brainstorming.
Bottom-up (starting from the stack trace) or top-down (starting from the input and follow the code flow)

Debugging log is great to write incident retrospectives

Incident retrospectives are a good practice to learn from past incidents, and share knowledge inside your team.

I need to iterate on cumulative lists:

[0], [0, 1], [0, 1, 2], ...

I need to add a special value at the beginning of each list:

-100 


                    def prepend(l: list, beginning: list = [-100]):
                        beginning.extend(l)
                        return beginning


                    for i in range(5):
                        zero_to_i = list(range(i))
                        l = prepend(zero_to_i)

                    print(len(l))
                    # Expected: 5; i = 4, zero_to_i = [0, 1, 2, 3], l = [-100, 0, 1, 2, 3]
                    # Actual: 11
line 7 creates lists of increasing length. prepend adds a special value at the beginning of the list. The default is -100, but it is configurable to allow any list of values.

Experiment 5

Investigate what is happening with multiple successive calls to prepend.

Experiment 5


                    def prepend(l: list, beginning: list = [-100]):
                        print("l: ", l)
                        print("beginning: ", beginning)
                        beginning.extend(l)
                        return beginning

                    prepend([0, 1, 2])
                    # l: [0, 1, 2]
                    # beginning: [-100]

                    prepend([0, 1, 2])
                    # l: [0, 1, 2]
                    # beginning: [-100, 0, 1, 2]

What is happening

  • Default arguments are instantiated once.
  • If a default argument is changed, the new value will be used as default for subsequent calls.
    • beginning.extend(l)
  • DO NOT use mutable default arguments.
Default arguments are created once, when the function is defined. If a default argument is changed during the function call, the new value will be used as default for subsequent calls. It is recommended not to use mutable default arguments. (Mutable == that can be modified, things like list and dict; immutable, tuples, strings and integers)

Fixed code


                    def prepend(l: list, beginning: list | None = None):
                        if beginning is None:
                            beginning = [-100]
                        beginning.extend(l)
                        return beginning

                        l = prepend(zero_to_i)

                    # Actual: 5

Other useful tools for your adventure

For the rest of the talk, I'd like to cover more practical advice on how to tackle a real bug, when your code base is not 10-line long, but more like 100k lines. How to build your theoretical view of your code, how to test your hypotheses efficiently, what tools can help you for your experiments... and how you can leverage your debugging log to guide you through your exploration.

Qualify the bug

  • What is the "bug"?
  • Is it a "bug"?
  • Is it reproducible?
First thing first, start your analysis with describing the bug: do you have a detailed description of the event? Expected behaviour matters (cf in bug report). Unsupported Python versions are not a bug. Can you find a point in time / a previous version of your code when the code was working as expected?

Leverage temporality

When was the last it was working?

What changed in between?

It was working before. There exists no ill-intentioned goblin who cursed your code. Think about what has changed (upgrades in your code, or in a dependency). git bisect can help narrow down the exact incriminated commit. Monitoring tooling can highlight a change in resource usage.

Identify source of randomness

Control what you can

Be mindful of what you don't

Code is rational, so if your experiments are not reproducible, but should be, you have not taken into account something. Sometimes randomness is out of our control (external API calls). Sometimes, you can fix the random seed to help with debugging. Sometimes, something is stateful when you thought you had no side effect.

Invest in tooling

Learn how to use a debugger

Set up some monitoring

Learn how to use a debugger. pdb might seem intimidating, and is not terribly user-friendly, but it is useful. As a team, invest in logging, and other monitoring tools: good logs can already validate or invalidate hypotheses, metrics can help in hard-to-reproduce scenarios Good monitoring solutions can help you gain some time, because it will narrow down your assumptions and hypotheses without running any experiments. If you have incidents in production, OOM killed machines, performance issues, it might be hard to reproduce outside a production environment. Metrics will be helpful to identify chain failures / temporary overload.

Enroll another adventurer

Debugging is a team effort

Work in pair / learn to ask good technical questions Synthesize your current level of knowledge to share with a coworker. Write a message to someone when you're stuck -> just exposing everything will highlight what you've missed / neglected to test.

Architect your maze better

Write code easy to debug

No one ever thought about that ^^ Not a reactive measure though... In a nutshell, code that is easy to test (stateless (FP), small & well-isolated components) is easier to debug.

The science of debugging in 3 principles

  • There's a logical explanation.
  • Understand what happens, do not fix the bug.
  • Trust nothing, check all your assumptions.
Like thermodynamic, debugging has 3 fundamental principles.

And use a debugging log to track your efforts
